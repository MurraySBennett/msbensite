# Background

How does expertise change what you see? The question sounds philosophical but it has a precise, tractable form in the study of numeral recognition. Arabic digits are among the most widely encountered symbols on earth, and yet speakers of different languages have quite different histories with them. A native English speaker and a native Chinese speaker have both used Arabic numerals their whole lives — but only the Chinese speaker has equivalent experience with Chinese numerals. Thai numerals are unfamiliar to both.

This creates a natural experimental design for studying how cultural expertise shapes mental representations: the same stimuli, presented to groups with systematically different exposure histories, under conditions that prevent strategic responding. The question becomes: when two people see the same numeral, are they perceiving the same thing?

# Analyses & Methodology

## Task Design

11 English-speaking and 11 Chinese-speaking participants each completed four testing sessions, identifying noisy versions of numerals from four systems:

- **Arabic digits** (0–9): familiar to both groups
- **Chinese numerals**: highly familiar to Chinese speakers, unfamiliar to English speakers
- **Thai numerals**: unfamiliar to both groups
- **Canonical dot patterns** (1–9): non-symbolic, representing quantity without learned symbol systems

Stimuli were degraded with visual noise to create perceptual uncertainty, and responses were made using a symbolic response wheel — a circular display of all possible numerals from which participants selected their answer. This response format was used specifically because it allows bias-free analysis: participants could respond with any numeral, but the *pattern* of confusions across numerals reveals the underlying similarity structure.

## Bias-Free Confusion Analysis

Confusion matrices built from raw response frequencies conflate two things: true perceptual similarity (how much 3 and 8 look alike) and response bias (a general tendency to pick 3 more often). A participant who always tends toward 3 will make 3 look perceptually similar to everything, which is not a claim about perception.

We applied **Luce's choice model** to separate these contributions, deriving bias-free confusion matrices that reflect only the perceptual similarity structure — how often stimuli are genuinely mistaken for each other after accounting for response tendencies.

## Multidimensional Scaling and Clustering

Bias-free confusion matrices were submitted to **multidimensional scaling (MDS)**, which maps the numeral systems into a low-dimensional perceptual space where distances correspond to confusability. Hierarchical clustering was then applied to identify natural groupings. Comparing the resulting spaces across English and Chinese cohorts reveals where cultural expertise leaves its mark on the geometry of perception.

# Outcomes & Conclusions

## What Doesn't Change: Non-Symbolic and Unfamiliar Systems

**Dot patterns** were represented nearly identically by both groups, with clustering reflecting numerical proximity and perceptual features (presence of a central dot, arrangements into rows). Quantity is quantity: the mental organisation of dot numerals does not depend on cultural background.

**Thai numerals** — unfamiliar to both participant groups — also produced similar representations across cultures. Without the scaffolding of extensive experience, both groups organised these symbols primarily by visual similarity: curved shapes grouped together, vertical strokes grouped together.

## What Changes: Expertise Reshapes Perception

**Arabic digits** were represented somewhat differently across groups despite being familiar to both. Subtle cross-cultural differences emerged in the perceptual space, likely reflecting differences in the typographic and contextual forms of Arabic digits that each group has encountered.

**Chinese numerals** showed the most striking divergence. English speakers, who have no systematic exposure to Chinese numerals, organised them by surface visual features. Chinese speakers organised them in a way that reflects their learned knowledge of the numeral system — structural and semantic properties of the symbols, not just their appearance. The same visual input was genuinely perceived differently by the two groups.

## Methodology as Contribution

Beyond the findings, the approach — large-scale bias-free confusion data plus multidimensional scaling — provides a template for studying how expertise alters mental representations in any domain. The method works wherever stimuli can be confused with each other under noise, and wherever the researcher needs to separate perceptual similarity from response bias. Applications extend to medical image interpretation, typeface design, signage, and any domain where recognition errors have practical consequences.

## Conclusion

Cultural expertise in numeral systems reshapes how those numerals are mentally organised. Unfamiliar symbols are represented by visual similarity across cultures; familiar symbols carry the imprint of experience. The study demonstrates that perceptual representations are not fixed by the visual input — they are actively shaped by what we know.
